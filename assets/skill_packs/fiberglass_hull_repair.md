# Fiberglass & Hull Repair

## Fiberglass Basics

Fiberglass (FRP — Fiber Reinforced Plastic) is layers of glass fiber cloth saturated with resin. The glass provides strength, the resin holds it together and provides a waterproof matrix.

**Types of resin:**
- **Polyester:** Cheapest, adequate for most repairs, strong smell, 2% MEKP catalyst by volume
- **Vinylester:** Better water resistance, preferred for below-waterline and blister repair
- **Epoxy:** Strongest, best adhesion, best water resistance, most expensive. Two-part (resin + hardener). West System and TotalBoat are popular marine brands.

**Types of glass cloth:**
- **Chopped strand mat (CSM):** Random fibers, builds thickness quickly, use with polyester only
- **Woven roving:** Bidirectional cloth, good strength, moderate cost
- **Biaxial cloth:** Two layers at ±45°, excellent for structural repairs
- **E-glass tape:** Narrow strip for tabbing and edge work

**IMPORTANT:** Epoxy resin will NOT bond to cured polyester mat that has a wax (styrene) surface. Sand thoroughly first. Epoxy bonds well to sanded polyester/vinylester.

## Gelcoat Repair

Gelcoat is the hard, glossy outer surface of a fiberglass boat. It's a specialized polyester resin with pigment.

### Scratches and Scuffs
1. Clean the area with acetone
2. Wet-sand with 400-grit, then 600-grit sandpaper
3. Polish with rubbing compound (3M Finesse-it or equivalent)
4. Apply wax to protect

### Chips and Gouges
1. Clean the damaged area with acetone
2. Sand the edges of the chip with 220-grit to create a slight V-groove (better adhesion)
3. Mask around the repair with tape
4. Mix gelcoat with catalyst (1-2% MEKP) and color-matched pigment
5. Apply with a putty knife, slightly overfilling (gelcoat shrinks slightly)
6. Cover with a piece of plastic wrap (PVA film or Saran wrap) — gelcoat needs to cure without air exposure
7. Let cure for 24 hours
8. Remove plastic, wet-sand with 400 → 600 → 1000 → 1500 grit
9. Polish with compound, then wax

**TIP:** Gelcoat won't cure properly if exposed to air (tacky surface). The plastic wrap is essential, or use gelcoat with wax additive (surfacing agent).

### Crazing (Spider Web Cracks)
Fine cracks in the gelcoat that don't penetrate the laminate. Usually cosmetic but can admit water.

1. Open the cracks slightly with a Dremel or sharp tool (V-groove)
2. Clean with acetone
3. Fill with catalyzed gelcoat or epoxy filler
4. Cover with plastic wrap for cure
5. Sand and polish smooth

## Fiberglass Layup Repair

### Small Hole Repair (Up to 3 inches)
1. **Assess:** Is the damage through the laminate? Check from inside.
2. **Clean:** Remove all damaged, delaminated, or wet material. Grind back to solid laminate.
3. **Bevel:** Grind a 12:1 taper ratio around the hole (for a 1/4" thick laminate, bevel 3 inches out from the edge)
4. **Back the hole** from the inside with a piece of stiff cardboard or plywood covered with release film (waxed paper or packing tape)
5. **Cut glass cloth:** Cut pieces in graduating sizes — smallest first (to fill the hole), then larger pieces to cover the bevel
6. **Mix resin:** Follow manufacturer's ratios exactly. Work time is typically 15-30 minutes at 70°F.
7. **Lay up:** Wet out each piece of cloth with resin, apply from smallest to largest. Use a roller or brush to remove air bubbles.
8. **Cure:** Allow full cure (24-48 hours at room temperature)
9. **Fair:** Sand with 80-grit, apply fairing compound (epoxy + microballoons), sand smooth with 120 → 220 grit
10. **Gelcoat:** Apply gelcoat and polish, or paint

### Delamination Repair
When layers of fiberglass separate from each other. Often caused by water intrusion or impact.

**Signs:** Soft or spongy areas, discoloration, hollow sound when tapped.

1. **Drill small holes** into the delaminated area (3/16" bit, every 2 inches)
2. **Dry the area** thoroughly — heat guns, dehumidifier, sun exposure. This may take days.
3. **Inject epoxy** into the holes using a syringe. Use low-viscosity epoxy for best penetration.
4. **Clamp or weight** the repair to compress layers together
5. **Allow full cure**
6. **Fill drill holes** with thickened epoxy, fair, and finish

## Osmotic Blistering

Water molecules pass through the gelcoat (osmosis), react with chemicals in the laminate, and form acidic fluid pockets that push up blisters.

### Diagnosis
- Small dome-shaped blisters (1/4" to 2") on the bottom
- Pop one: if the fluid inside smells like vinegar (acetic acid), it's osmotic
- Untreated, blisters grow and multiply — eventually weakening the laminate

### Repair Process
1. **Haul the boat** — this is a haul-out job
2. **Remove all bottom paint** (sanding, chemical stripper, or blasting)
3. **Open every blister** — grind each one down to dry laminate
4. **Wash thoroughly** with fresh water and allow to dry (may take weeks in a covered shed)
5. **Test moisture** with a moisture meter — the hull must read dry before proceeding
6. **Fill blisters** with epoxy filler (West System 410 Microlight or similar)
7. **Fair** the surface smooth
8. **Apply barrier coat** — 5-6 coats of high-build epoxy (Interprotect, TotalBoat TotalProtect)
9. **Apply bottom paint** over the barrier coat

**Prevention:** A new boat should have barrier coat applied before launching. Cheaper than fixing blisters later.

## Core Rot

Many boat decks (and some hulls) use a sandwich construction: fiberglass skins with a lightweight core (balsa wood or closed-cell foam) in between.

### Identification
- Deck feels soft or spongy when walked on
- Visible discoloration or water weeping from fittings
- Tapping test: sounds dull/dead instead of crisp/hard

### Repair
1. **Remove the top skin** over the damaged area. Cut with an oscillating tool or router — just deep enough to reach the core. Be careful not to cut through the bottom skin.
2. **Remove all wet/rotted core** — dig it out with a chisel. Go until you reach dry, solid core material.
3. **Dry the cavity** — let it air dry for several days, or use a heat gun carefully
4. **Cut new core** to fit — balsa or Divinycell foam (closed-cell). Corecell is excellent.
5. **Bed the new core** in thickened epoxy (epoxy + colloidal silica to peanut butter consistency)
6. **Replace the top skin:** Lay up new fiberglass over the core — minimum 2-3 layers of biaxial cloth or equivalent to match original thickness
7. **Fair and finish**

**CRITICAL:** When rebedding deck hardware, always seal the bolt holes completely with sealant to prevent water from reaching the core. Every deck leak eventually causes core rot.

## Keel Attachment Inspection

The keel is bolted through the hull. A loose keel can be catastrophic.

### Annual Inspection
1. Check keel bolts from inside the boat (under the floorboards)
2. Look for rust staining, weeping water, or cracked filler around bolt heads
3. Check torque if possible — bolts should be tight (consult manufacturer specs)
4. Inspect the keel-to-hull joint externally — any separation or cracking?
5. On grounding: always inspect keel bolts afterward

**WARNING:** Keel bolt failure while sailing can result in immediate capsize and sinking. Take any signs of keel bolt problems seriously.

## Rudder Inspection

### External Check (While Hauled)
1. Tap the rudder all over — should sound solid and consistent
2. Check for blisters, soft spots, or water weeping
3. Inspect the rudder stock where it enters the hull
4. Check pintles and gudgeons (if skeg-hung) for wear and play
5. Wiggle the rudder — any excessive play indicates worn bearings

### Water in the Rudder
Many rudders have a foam core. Water intrusion adds weight and eventually destroys the core.
- Drill a small drain hole at the lowest point while hauled
- If water drains out: the rudder needs professional assessment
- A waterlogged rudder may need to be rebuilt or replaced

## Working with Epoxy

### Mixing
- **Always follow the manufacturer's ratio exactly** (by volume or weight, as specified)
- West System 105/205: 5 parts resin to 1 part hardener (by volume) — use pumps for accuracy
- Mix thoroughly for at least 2 minutes, scraping sides and bottom
- Mix only what you can use in the working time

### Temperature Requirements
- Ideal working temperature: 65-80°F (18-27°C)
- Below 60°F: cure is extremely slow or may not complete
- Above 90°F: cure is too fast, may generate excessive heat (exotherm)
- The surface being bonded must also be above 60°F

### Thickening Agents
Add to mixed epoxy for different purposes:
- **Colloidal silica (Cab-O-Sil):** Strong structural filler, non-sagging
- **Microfibers:** High-strength filler for bonding
- **Microballoons:** Lightweight, easy to sand — use for fairing only (not structural)
- **Wood flour:** Good for bonding wood to fiberglass

### Safety
- **Wear gloves** — nitrile, not latex (epoxy sensitization is permanent)
- **Ventilation** — work in fresh air
- **Eye protection** — uncured epoxy can cause severe eye irritation
- **Skin contact:** If you develop a rash, you may be sensitized. Once sensitized, you cannot work with epoxy again without severe reactions.

## Sanding, Fairing, and Finishing

### Sanding Guide
| Grit | Use |
|------|-----|
| 36-60 | Heavy material removal, grinding old paint |
| 80-120 | Shaping, initial fairing, preparing for primer |
| 150-220 | Smoothing before gelcoat or paint |
| 320-400 | Between coats of paint or varnish |
| 600-1000 | Wet-sanding gelcoat before polishing |
| 1500-2000 | Final wet-sand before compound polishing |

### Fairing Compound
For making surfaces smooth and fair:
1. Mix epoxy with microballoons to a thick, smooth paste
2. Apply with a wide putty knife or spreader
3. Build up in layers if needed (let each layer kick before adding more)
4. Sand when cured with a long board (prevents creating dips)
5. The surface should feel perfectly smooth under a straightedge

## Emergency Hull Repair at Sea

### Impact Damage / Hole Below Waterline
1. **Reduce water ingress:** Stuff the hole with cushions, towels, rags
2. **Deploy bilge pumps** — all of them
3. **From outside:** If accessible, cover with a collision mat, sail bag, or tarp. Water pressure helps hold external patches.
4. **From inside:** Once flow is reduced, apply underwater epoxy (MarineTex, West System G/Flex)
5. **Brace with plywood** and whatever lumber is available
6. **Head for the nearest port**

### Underwater Epoxy Application
Products like West System G/Flex 655 or MarineTex cure underwater:
1. Knead the two parts together (wearing gloves)
2. Surface doesn't need to be dry (but remove loose debris)
3. Press firmly against the hull, hold for 2-3 minutes
4. For larger areas: apply epoxy, cover with fiberglass cloth, apply more epoxy on top
5. Initial cure: 30-60 minutes. Full strength: 24 hours.

## Anti-Fouling Paint

### Types
- **Ablative (self-polishing):** Wears away slowly, releasing biocide. Good for boats with variable use.
- **Hard/modified epoxy:** Tough surface, can be burnished smooth. Good for racing boats or boats that sit in the water year-round.
- **Copper-based:** Most common biocide. Don't use on aluminum hulls (galvanic corrosion).

### Application
1. Remove old loose or flaking paint (sanding or scraping)
2. Apply barrier coat if bare gelcoat (2-3 coats of epoxy barrier)
3. Apply anti-fouling per manufacturer's instructions (typically 2 coats)
4. Apply an extra coat at the waterline and on running gear (higher fouling areas)
5. Launch within the paint manufacturer's recommended window after application
