# Corrosion & Fasteners

## Why Corrosion Matters on Boats

Corrosion is the #1 long-term enemy of every boat. The marine environment — salt water, salt air, moisture, and dissimilar metals in contact — creates the perfect conditions for metal to deteriorate. Understanding corrosion prevents catastrophic failures.

## Types of Marine Corrosion

### Galvanic Corrosion
Occurs when two different metals are in contact in the presence of an electrolyte (salt water). The less noble (more reactive) metal corrodes to protect the more noble one.

**The Galvanic Series (marine — simplified, most noble to least):**
1. Titanium, Gold, Platinum (most noble — won't corrode)
2. Stainless steel 316 (passive)
3. Monel, nickel alloys
4. Bronze, copper, brass
5. Tin
6. Lead
7. Stainless steel 316 (active — in oxygen-depleted crevices)
8. Cast iron, steel
9. Aluminum alloys
10. Zinc
11. Magnesium (least noble — corrodes fastest)

**Key rule:** Metals far apart on this list corrode aggressively when in contact. Metals close together are compatible.

**Common galvanic corrosion scenarios:**
- Stainless steel bolt in aluminum mast = aluminum corrodes around the bolt
- Bronze through-hull with steel hose clamp = steel clamp disintegrates
- Copper bottom paint on aluminum hull = aluminum hull dissolves

**Prevention:**
- Use metals close together on the galvanic series
- Isolate dissimilar metals with plastic washers, Tefgel, or dielectric compound
- Install sacrificial anodes (zinc or aluminum) to protect more noble metals

### Crevice Corrosion
Occurs in tight gaps where water enters but oxygen is limited: under washers, in joints, inside swage fittings, behind hardware.

Stainless steel is particularly vulnerable — it relies on an oxide layer that needs oxygen to maintain. In a crevice, oxygen is depleted and the stainless becomes "active" (loses its protective layer).

**Prevention:**
- Bed all hardware properly with sealant (no gaps for water to enter)
- Use open-body turnbuckles (allow water drainage and inspection)
- Inspect swage fittings for hairline cracks
- Don't let debris accumulate in crevices

### Electrolysis (Stray Current Corrosion)
Caused by electrical current flowing through the water between metals. Much more destructive than galvanic corrosion. Can destroy a propeller or shaft in weeks.

**Signs:** Rapid metal loss, pitting, underwater metals disappearing unusually fast, zinc anodes consumed very quickly (weeks instead of months).

**Common causes:**
- Damaged wiring allowing current to leak to ground
- Faulty shore power connections
- Neighboring boat with wiring problems (marina-wide issue)
- Bilge pump wiring submerged in bilge water

**Diagnosis:**
1. Disconnect shore power and all battery switches
2. Use a multimeter: measure DC voltage between your underwater metals and the water. Should be near 0V. Anything over 0.05V indicates a problem.
3. Reconnect one system at a time to identify the source

**Prevention:**
- Maintain proper wiring (no bare wires in the bilge)
- Use a galvanic isolator or isolation transformer on shore power
- Check for stray current annually with a silver/silver-chloride reference electrode

### Dezincification (Brass and Bronze)
Zinc leaches out of the alloy, leaving soft, porous, pink copper behind. Affects brass and some bronzes.

**Signs:** Metal turns pink/salmon color, becomes soft and brittle.

**Affected materials:** Yellow brass (common brass), manganese bronze (despite the name, it's actually a brass and is susceptible)

**Safe materials:** Silicon bronze, true gunmetal bronze, Aquamet

**Rule: Never use yellow brass below the waterline.** Use only true bronze, silicon bronze, or Marelon (composite).

## Sacrificial Anodes (Zincs)

Sacrificial anodes protect your boat's underwater metals by corroding preferentially.

### Anode Materials
- **Zinc:** Traditional, good for salt water. Not effective in fresh water.
- **Aluminum:** Works in both salt and fresh water. Becoming the standard.
- **Magnesium:** Fresh water only. Too reactive for salt water (wastes away too fast).

### Where Anodes Are Needed
- **Shaft anode (collar)** — clamps around the prop shaft
- **Rudder anode** — bolted to the rudder
- **Hull anodes** — bolted to the hull (especially near through-hulls)
- **Engine anode (pencil zinc)** — inside the heat exchanger
- **Propeller anode** — on the prop nut or behind the prop

### Inspection Rules
- Check every haul-out (minimum annually)
- Replace when **50% consumed** — don't wait until they're gone
- If anodes are consumed much faster than expected → check for stray current
- If anodes are NOT consumed at all → they may not have good electrical contact (check the bonding wire)

### Installation
- Anodes MUST have direct metal-to-metal contact or a bonding wire connected to the bonding system
- Don't paint over anodes — paint insulates them from the water
- Use the correct size for your boat (follow manufacturer recommendations)

## Fastener Guide for Marine Use

### Fastener Materials — What to Use Where

**316 Stainless Steel** (best general-purpose marine fastener):
- Good corrosion resistance in salt air and splash zone
- Adequate above the waterline in most situations
- Caution: susceptible to crevice corrosion underwater — not ideal for permanent submerged use
- Magnetic test: 316 is slightly magnetic; 304 is less so. True 316 should be only very weakly attracted to a magnet.

**Silicon Bronze** (best for wood boats, excellent below waterline):
- Exceptional corrosion resistance in salt water
- Traditional boat-building fastener
- Available as screws, bolts, and rivets
- Expensive but worth it for critical applications

**Monel** (premium):
- Nickel-copper alloy, extremely corrosion-resistant
- Used for critical underwater fasteners, shaft keys, propeller hardware
- Very expensive

**Aluminum** (for aluminum boats/masts):
- Use aluminum fasteners in aluminum structure to avoid galvanic corrosion
- 5000 or 6000 series marine-grade aluminum

**Brass** — AVOID below the waterline (dezincification risk)

**Mild steel / zinc-plated** — AVOID in the marine environment (temporary use only)

### Fastener Selection Rules
1. **Match the fastener to the material it's going into** (same metal = no galvanic corrosion)
2. If you must mix metals: use the more noble metal as the fastener (it's smaller and won't corrode the larger piece)
3. Use isolation washers/Tefgel when mixing metals
4. **Below waterline:** Silicon bronze, Monel, or properly isolated 316 stainless
5. **Above waterline:** 316 stainless for most applications
6. **In the mast:** 316 stainless or aluminum (match the mast material)

## Dealing with Seized and Corroded Fasteners

### Prevention
- Apply anti-seize compound (Tefgel, Tef-Gel, or Lanocote) to all fasteners during installation
- Use Tef-Gel (not regular anti-seize) for stainless-to-stainless connections — it prevents galling

### Removing Seized Bolts — Step by Step

**Level 1 — Penetrating Oil:**
1. Apply penetrating oil (PB Blaster, Kroil, or 50/50 acetone + ATF)
2. Let it soak for 30-60 minutes (overnight is better)
3. Tap the bolt head gently with a hammer (vibration helps oil penetrate)
4. Try turning — apply firm, steady force (don't round the head)

**Level 2 — Heat:**
1. Apply penetrating oil first, then heat the SURROUNDING MATERIAL (not the bolt)
2. Use a propane torch or heat gun — heat causes the surrounding material to expand, breaking the corrosion bond
3. **NEVER heat near fuel, gas, or flammable materials**
4. Heat until the surrounding metal changes color slightly (~300°F)
5. Let it cool slightly, apply more penetrating oil, try turning

**Level 3 — Impact:**
1. Use an impact driver (hand-powered or electric)
2. The combination of rotational force and hammering action breaks the corrosion
3. Start with the loosening direction — reverse the impact driver setting

**Level 4 — Extraction:**
If the bolt head rounds off or the bolt breaks:
1. **Bolt extractor (EZ-Out):** Drill a small hole in the bolt center, insert the extractor, turn counterclockwise
2. **Left-hand drill bit:** Sometimes the drilling action alone backs the bolt out
3. **Weld a nut onto the broken bolt** (if accessible) — then turn the nut
4. **Drill it out:** Use a drill bit slightly smaller than the bolt thread diameter, drill through, clean threads with a tap

### Galling Prevention (Stainless-to-Stainless)
Stainless steel bolts in stainless steel threads are prone to galling — the threads weld together during tightening and seize permanently.

**Prevention:**
- Always use Tefgel or anti-seize on stainless-to-stainless threads
- Don't use power tools to tighten — hand-tighten to control torque
- Use slow, even force when tightening and loosening
- If a stainless bolt starts to resist: STOP, back out, apply more anti-seize, try again

## Bedding Fasteners in Deck Hardware

Every bolt hole through the deck is a potential water intrusion point leading to core rot.

### Proper Bedding Procedure
1. **Dry-fit** all hardware first, mark and drill holes
2. **Seal the core:** After drilling, fill the hole with unthickened epoxy (syringe). Let cure. Re-drill. This seals the exposed core material from water.
3. **Apply sealant** (butyl tape or polyurethane — see Plumbing skill pack for sealant selection)
4. **Insert fasteners** through sealant
5. **Tighten to snug** (not fully tight)
6. **Wait 30-60 minutes** for sealant to begin setting
7. **Final tighten**
8. **Clean excess sealant**

### Re-Bedding Existing Hardware
1. Remove fasteners
2. Carefully pry hardware off — cut through sealant with a putty knife
3. Clean all old sealant from hardware and deck (acetone or scraper)
4. Inspect the deck core (check for soft spots — probe with a pick)
5. If core is wet/soft: repair the core BEFORE re-bedding (see Fiberglass skill pack)
6. Re-bed using the procedure above

## Corrosion Inspection Checklist

### Annual (or Every Haul-Out)
- [ ] Check all sacrificial anodes — replace if >50% consumed
- [ ] Inspect prop shaft for pitting or discoloration
- [ ] Check propeller for pitting, dezincification (pink spots)
- [ ] Inspect rudder hardware
- [ ] Check all through-hull fittings (dezincification on brass? replace with bronze)
- [ ] Inspect keel bolts (from inside)
- [ ] Check bonding system continuity with a multimeter
- [ ] Test for stray current with shore power connected and disconnected

### Topside
- [ ] Check chainplate fittings and surrounding area
- [ ] Inspect all deck hardware fasteners
- [ ] Check stanchion bases and pulpit fittings
- [ ] Inspect winch mounting bolts
- [ ] Check rigging terminals (swage fittings) for cracks or staining
- [ ] Inspect any aluminum-to-stainless connections (mast fittings)
