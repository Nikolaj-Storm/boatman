# Marine Plumbing & Water Systems — Complete Guide

## Through-Hulls and Seacocks

Through-hulls are openings in the hull below the waterline. Each one is a potential sinking hazard. Every sailor must know where every through-hull is located and how to close it.

### Types of Seacocks

**Ball valve (modern):**
- Quarter-turn operation (handle parallel to hose = open, perpendicular = closed)
- Most common on modern boats
- Marelon (composite) or bronze

**Gate valve (avoid):**
- Multi-turn operation like a faucet
- NOT recommended for below-waterline use — can fail to seal
- If your boat has gate valves below the waterline, replace them with ball valves

**Traditional tapered plug (classic):**
- Bronze, requires regular greasing
- Very reliable when maintained

### Through-Hull Maintenance

**Every haul-out (annually at minimum):**
1. Exercise all seacocks — open and close fully several times
2. Inspect for corrosion (bronze turns pink = dezincification = REPLACE)
3. Check hose connections — double-clamped with stainless hose clamps?
4. Inspect hoses for cracking, softness, or swelling
5. Replace any questionable hoses — they are cheap compared to sinking

**CRITICAL RULE:** Every through-hull must have a properly-sized wooden plug tied to it or nearby. If a through-hull fails, a tapered softwood plug driven into the hole will stop the flooding.

### Emergency Through-Hull Failure

**If a through-hull fitting breaks or a hose comes loose below the waterline:**

1. **Stay calm.** You have time — most through-hulls are 3/4" to 1.5" diameter.
2. **Close the seacock** if the body is intact
3. If the seacock is broken: **Drive a wooden plug** into the opening
4. If no plug available: Use a towel, rag, cushion — anything to reduce flow
5. **Start the bilge pump** (manual and electric)
6. **Call for help** on VHF Channel 16 if the situation is not quickly controlled

**ALWAYS know where your wooden plugs are. Check them during every safety briefing.**

## Fresh Water System

### System Components
1. **Water tank(s)** — Aluminum, polyethylene, or fiberglass
2. **Pressure pump** — Electric diaphragm pump (runs automatically when a faucet opens)
3. **Accumulator tank** — Small pressure tank that reduces pump cycling
4. **Manual pump** — Foot or hand pump (backup, and for conserving water)
5. **Water heater** — Usually heated by engine coolant loop and/or shore power element
6. **Filters** — Inline carbon or sediment filters

### Pressure Pump Troubleshooting

**Pump runs but no water flows:**
1. Check that the tank is not empty
2. Check for a clogged inlet strainer on the pump
3. Check for an airlock — open the farthest faucet and let it run
4. Check that hose connections are secure (air leak prevents suction)

**Pump cycles on and off without faucets open:**
1. **Small leak somewhere in the system** — Check all connections, faucets, and the water heater
2. **Failed check valve in the pump** — Replace the pump or its check valve
3. **Waterlogged accumulator tank** — Re-pressurize (typically 20 PSI) or replace bladder

**Pump won't run at all:**
1. Check the fuse/breaker
2. Check power at the pump (use multimeter)
3. Check the pressure switch — it may be stuck
4. Check for a clogged filter reducing flow

### Water Conservation at Sea

When cruising with limited water:
- Use a foot pump for hand washing (50% less water than pressure pump)
- Navy showers: wet down, soap up with water off, rinse quickly
- Catch rainwater when possible (use clean awnings/tarps)
- Saltwater for dishes (first wash), freshwater for final rinse
- Monitor tank levels daily

## Marine Head (Toilet) Systems

### Manual Marine Head — How It Works

A manual marine head uses a hand pump to flush. The pump pulls seawater in and pushes waste out. A Y-valve directs waste either overboard (where legal) or to a holding tank.

**Key components:**
1. **Pump handle** — The piston pump that moves water and waste
2. **Intake seacock** — Raw water supply for flushing
3. **Joker valve** — A one-way rubber valve (duck-bill shape) that prevents backflow
4. **Discharge seacock** or hose to holding tank
5. **Y-valve** — Switches between overboard discharge and holding tank

### Head Maintenance and Common Problems

**Blocked head (won't pump):**
1. **Most common cause:** Calcium buildup from seawater. Prevent by pumping 10+ full strokes of clean water after each use.
2. Check for foreign objects (NOTHING goes in except human waste and single-ply toilet paper — NO wipes, NO tampons, NO paper towels)
3. Remove the discharge hose and check for blockage
4. Disassemble the pump and clean

**Head smells bad (even when clean):**
1. **Permeated hoses** — Sanitation hoses absorb odor over time. Replace all head hoses every 5-7 years. Use only proper sanitation hose (not regular water hose).
2. Wipe the suspect hose with a hot wet rag — if the rag smells, the hose needs replacing
3. Check holding tank vent for blockage
4. Check that the joker valve is sealing properly

### Joker Valve Replacement

The joker valve prevents waste from flowing back into the bowl. Replace annually or when the bowl no longer holds water.

**Procedure:**
1. Close both seacocks (intake and discharge)
2. Pump the head dry
3. Locate the valve — usually in the pump discharge fitting
4. Remove the discharge hose from the pump
5. Pull out the old joker valve with needle-nose pliers
6. Insert the new valve (open end faces away from the pump — check arrow)
7. Reconnect the hose with double clamps
8. Open seacocks and test

### Holding Tank Management

**Pump-out procedure:**
- Use marina pump-out stations
- Connect the pump-out nozzle to the deck fitting
- Open the holding tank vent
- Pump until empty
- Rinse with fresh water if possible

**Holding tank vent:**
- Must be unobstructed (check for spider webs/salt crystals)
- Install a vent filter to prevent odor
- A blocked vent prevents pump-out and can pressurize the tank

## Bilge Pump Systems

### Electric Bilge Pumps

Every boat should have at least two bilge pumps:
1. **Primary automatic pump** with float switch — turns on when water rises
2. **Manual override** — Can be activated from the helm

**Pump capacity:** For boats under 40ft, a minimum 1500 GPH rated pump. Note: actual output is much less than rated capacity due to head height.

### Float Switch Problems

The float switch automatically activates the bilge pump when water rises. It is the most failure-prone component.

**Common failures:**
1. **Stuck in the down position** — Debris, oil, or fouling prevents it from floating. Clean regularly.
2. **Stuck in the up position** — Pump runs continuously, drains battery
3. **Corroded connections** — Clean and seal with dielectric grease
4. **Wire chafe** — Inspect wiring run from switch to panel

**Maintenance:** Clean the bilge and float switch every month. Test by pouring water into the bilge.

### Manual Bilge Pump

A manual bilge pump (diaphragm type, deck-mounted) is your last line of defense. It requires no electricity and should be operable from the cockpit.

**Flow rate:** A good manual pump can move 10-15 gallons per minute with vigorous pumping.

**Test it regularly.** Make sure the handle is accessible and the hose is clear.

## Emergency Leak Repair

### Soft Wood Plugs
Keep a set of tapered softwood plugs in multiple sizes, tied near each through-hull. Pine is ideal — it swells when wet, improving the seal.

### Underwater Epoxy
Two-part marine epoxy (like West System G/Flex or MarineTex) can cure underwater. Useful for:
- Patching small hull cracks
- Sealing around a damaged through-hull
- Temporary repair of a weeping fitting

**Application:** Knead the two parts together, press firmly against the wet surface, hold for 2-3 minutes until it grips.

### Emergency Patches

**For small holes/cracks (up to 2-3 inches):**
1. Clean the area as much as possible
2. Apply underwater epoxy
3. Cover with a piece of fiberglass cloth if available
4. Apply more epoxy over the cloth
5. Brace or hold in place until cured (15-30 minutes for initial set)

**For larger breaches:**
1. **Collision mat technique:** Place a cushion, sail bag, or folded tarp over the hole from the outside. Water pressure helps hold it in place.
2. **Back up from inside** with plywood and bracing
3. **Use the bilge pumps** to manage water ingress
4. **Head for the nearest port** or call for assistance

### Stuffing Box / Stern Gland

The stuffing box (or stern gland) seals around the propeller shaft where it exits the hull. A small drip is normal — about 1-2 drops per minute when the shaft is turning.

**Too much drip (steady stream):**
1. Tighten the packing nut 1/8 turn at a time
2. Check after running the engine — should be dripping slowly
3. Don't over-tighten! This causes the shaft to overheat and can score the shaft

**Repacking the stuffing box:**
1. Haul the boat or work with the boat partially lifted
2. Remove the packing nut and lock nut
3. Extract old packing with a packing removal tool (bent wire)
4. Count the rings of old packing (usually 3-5 rings)
5. Cut new packing material to shaft diameter (wrap around shaft, cut to length)
6. Install rings with joints staggered 90-120 degrees apart
7. Reassemble, tighten to finger-tight plus 1/4 turn
8. Test under power and adjust

## Sealants and Adhesives Guide

### When to Use What

**3M 5200 (Permanent):**
- Structural bonding — hull fittings, keel bolts
- Through-hulls (if you never want to remove them)
- VERY difficult to remove once cured
- **Do NOT use** on hardware you'll need to service

**3M 4200 (Semi-permanent):**
- Deck hardware that may need to be removed
- Ports and windows
- Handrails and cleats
- Removable with effort

**Silicone (RTV):**
- Gaskets and seals
- Areas that need flexibility
- Not structural — low adhesion
- Easy to remove
- **Don't use** below the waterline

**Butyl tape/sealant:**
- Deck hardware bedding
- Port lights
- Easy to work with, easy to remove
- Good for large area sealing
- Not waterproof under pressure

**Polyurethane (Sikaflex, BoatLife):**
- Similar to 4200/5200 depending on grade
- Good UV resistance
- Flexible when cured

### Proper Bedding Procedure

When mounting deck hardware:
1. Clean all surfaces with solvent (acetone or denatured alcohol)
2. Apply sealant to the base of the fitting AND around the bolt holes
3. Insert fasteners — snug but don't fully tighten
4. Let the sealant squeeze out and begin to skin (30-60 minutes)
5. Tighten fully
6. Clean excess sealant
7. Allow full cure time (24-72 hours depending on product)

**Key principle:** Sealant should fill the gap between surfaces. It is NOT glue — the fasteners hold the part on. The sealant keeps water out.

## Hose Clamp Standards

### Below Waterline
- Use **all stainless steel** hose clamps (band AND screw)
- **Double-clamped** — two clamps on each connection
- Inspect for rust staining annually (indicates low-quality stainless)
- Replace any clamp that shows corrosion

### Above Waterline
- Single stainless clamps are acceptable
- Still inspect annually

### Testing Hose Clamps
Squeeze the hose near the clamp. If the hose moves on the fitting, the clamp needs tightening. If the hose is hard, cracked, or swollen — replace the hose.

**Carry spare hose clamps in multiple sizes on board — they are one of the most useful items in your spare parts kit.**
