# Hydraulics & Hoses

## Hydraulic Systems on Boats

Most sailboats and many motorboats use hydraulic systems for steering, and often for autopilots, windlasses, bow thrusters, and trim tabs. Understanding the basics and knowing how to troubleshoot keeps you safe and in control.

## Hydraulic Steering Systems

### How It Works
A hydraulic steering system uses fluid pressure to move the rudder:
1. **Helm pump** — When you turn the wheel, a pump sends hydraulic fluid through a line
2. **Hydraulic lines** — High-pressure hoses or copper/steel tubing carry the fluid
3. **Steering cylinder (ram)** — Fluid pushes a piston that moves the tiller arm/quadrant
4. **Reservoir** — Stores extra fluid to compensate for temperature changes and minor leaks

### Types
- **Single-station:** One helm, one cylinder, simple loop
- **Multi-station:** Two or more helms (e.g., flybridge + cockpit), with helm lock valves
- **Power-assisted:** Engine-driven pump supplements manual effort (larger boats)

### Fluid Types
- **Most systems:** Dexron ATF (automatic transmission fluid) — red
- **Some European systems (Vetus, Teleflex):** Specific hydraulic oil — check your manual
- **NEVER mix fluid types** — incompatible fluids cause seal failure

## Hydraulic System Inspection

### Monthly Checks
1. **Check fluid level** in the reservoir (helm pump usually has a fill cap)
2. **Inspect all visible hoses** for chafing, cracking, bulging, or weeping
3. **Check fittings** for drips or staining
4. **Turn the wheel lock-to-lock** — should be smooth with consistent effort
5. **Check the steering cylinder** — look for fluid weeping around the rod seal

### Annual Checks
1. **Check fluid condition** — should be clear red (ATF) or amber (hydraulic oil). Dark, murky, or gritty fluid needs replacement.
2. **Inspect all hose runs** — look for chafe points where hoses pass through bulkheads or near sharp edges
3. **Check cylinder mount bolts** — must be tight
4. **Inspect the tiller arm/quadrant connection** — no play, no corrosion
5. **Test the rudder stops** — the cylinder should not bottom out before the mechanical stops engage

## Hydraulic Hose Inspection

### What to Look For

**Replace immediately if you see:**
- Fluid weeping or dripping from a hose or fitting
- Bulging or blistering in the hose wall
- Exposed wire braid (reinforcement layer visible through outer cover)
- Cracks in the outer cover (especially near fittings)
- Hard, stiff hose that won't flex (age-hardened)
- Hose showing twist (rotated during installation — stress point)

**Watch and plan replacement if you see:**
- Surface checking (fine cracks in the outer layer)
- Slight stiffness compared to new hose
- Hose older than 10 years (even if it looks OK)
- Chafe marks on the outer cover

### Hose Types
- **SAE 100R1** — Single wire braid, suitable for most marine steering (up to 2500 PSI working pressure)
- **SAE 100R2** — Double wire braid, higher pressure rating
- **Nylon tubing** — Used in some low-pressure systems (Teleflex/Seastar)
- **Copper tubing** — Traditional, durable, but can fatigue from vibration

**IMPORTANT:** When replacing a hose, match the specifications exactly. Pressure rating, inside diameter, fitting type, and length must be correct.

## Bleeding the Hydraulic Steering System

Air in the hydraulic system causes spongy or unresponsive steering. You must bleed air out whenever:
- You've replaced a hose or fitting
- The fluid level got too low
- The system has been opened for any reason

### Bleeding Procedure (Single Station)

1. **Top off the reservoir** with the correct fluid
2. **Remove the bleed screws** at the steering cylinder (there are usually two — one on each end)
3. **Turn the wheel slowly** from lock to lock, back and forth
4. As you turn, fluid with air bubbles will come out the bleed screws
5. **Keep the reservoir topped off** — never let it run dry during bleeding
6. Continue turning until fluid flows clear (no bubbles) from both bleed screws
7. **Tighten the bleed screws**
8. **Top off the reservoir** one final time
9. **Test:** Turn lock-to-lock several times. Steering should feel firm and consistent.
10. **Check for leaks** at all fittings and bleed screws

### Bleeding Procedure (Dual Station)
Dual station systems are more complex — each helm has its own pump:
1. Start with the secondary (less-used) helm
2. Lock the secondary helm with the helm lock valve
3. Bleed from the primary helm as described above
4. Switch: lock the primary, unlock the secondary
5. Bleed from the secondary helm
6. Open both helm locks and test from each station

### Persistent Sponginess After Bleeding
If steering remains spongy after bleeding:
- Check for a leak allowing air to enter (suction-side fittings can draw air in)
- Check seals on the helm pump — worn seals allow air past
- The cylinder rod seal may be worn — fluid leaks out, air enters when the ram retracts
- Small air pockets can be trapped in high points of the line run — route lines to avoid traps

## Autopilot Hydraulic Systems

Many autopilots use a small hydraulic pump to move the steering:
- **Linear drive** (ram type) — pushes the tiller arm directly
- **Rotary drive** — turns the steering wheel or pedestal

### Common Autopilot Hydraulic Issues
- **Pilot can't hold course:** Check fluid level, check for air in the system, check drive unit mounting
- **Pilot moves rudder slowly:** Low fluid, failing pump, restricted line
- **Pilot overshoots:** May be a control (gain) setting issue, not hydraulic
- **Noise from the drive unit:** Air in the system or worn pump

## Trim Tabs

Hydraulic trim tabs use small cylinders to adjust two plates on the transom:
- **Fluid type:** Usually dedicated trim tab fluid (check manufacturer)
- **Common issues:** Slow operation (low fluid or worn seals), tabs won't hold position (cylinder bypass or check valve failure)
- **Inspection:** Check for fluid weeping at cylinder rods, inspect hoses for chafe where they exit the hull

## Emergency Steering

### If Hydraulic Steering Fails Completely
1. **Do NOT panic** — you have options
2. **Engage the emergency tiller** (every boat with hydraulic steering should have one)
   - Usually fits over the top of the rudder stock, accessed through a cockpit locker or lazarette
   - Practice installing it BEFORE you need it
3. **Reduce sail/speed** — the boat will be harder to steer manually
4. If the failure is a burst hose: **close any isolation valves** to prevent total fluid loss
5. **Head for the nearest port** under emergency tiller control

### Emergency Hose Repair
If a hydraulic hose fails at sea:
1. **Plug both ends** immediately — use bolts that fit the fittings, or crimp/fold and clamp the hose ends
2. This converts you to emergency tiller steering
3. A temporary repair can be made by cutting out the damaged section and joining with a barbed fitting + hose clamps (NOT a permanent fix — get to port)

## Hose Replacement Guide

### Measuring for Replacement
1. **Inside diameter (ID):** Measure the hose bore, not the outside. Common sizes: 3/16", 1/4", 3/8", 1/2"
2. **Length:** Measure the old hose. Allow for gentle curves — kinking kills hoses
3. **Fittings:** Note the type (JIC/AN flare, NPT, ORB), size, and gender (male/female) at each end
4. **Pressure rating:** Check your system's working pressure (most steering: 1000-1500 PSI)

### Installation Tips
- **Don't twist the hose** during installation. Mark a line along the hose length to verify it hasn't rotated.
- **Support the hose** with clamps every 24 inches. Don't let it hang or rub on other components.
- **Allow slack** for steering movement — the hose must flex without pulling on fittings
- **Route away from heat** — keep hoses clear of the engine and exhaust
- **Protect against chafe** — use chafe guards where hoses pass through bulkheads

### Fitting Tightening
- **JIC/AN fittings:** Hand-tight, then wrench-tighten 1/6 turn (one flat). Over-tightening cracks the flare.
- **NPT fittings:** Use Teflon tape (PTFE) on the threads. Hand-tight plus 1.5-2 turns.
- **ORB fittings:** O-ring seal — tighten until the fitting shoulders against the port. Don't overtighten.

## Windlass Hydraulic Systems

Some anchor windlasses are hydraulically powered (common on larger boats):

### Common Issues
- **Windlass slow or weak:** Check fluid level, check for line restrictions, pump may be worn
- **Windlass won't run at all:** Check solenoid valve (electrical control), check pump clutch engagement
- **Overheating:** Windlass is overworked — allow cooling periods during long chain retrieval. Check fluid level.

### Maintenance
- Check fluid level monthly
- Change fluid per manufacturer schedule (typically every 500 hours or 2 years)
- Inspect hoses and fittings annually
- Keep the fluid reservoir clean — contamination kills pumps and valves
