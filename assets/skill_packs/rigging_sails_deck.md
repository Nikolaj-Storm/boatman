# Rigging, Sails & Deck Hardware

## Standing Rigging Inspection

Standing rigging holds the mast up. It doesn't move during normal sailing and includes shrouds, stays, and their fittings. Failure of any component can bring down the rig.

### What to Inspect

**Wire rope (1x19 or 7x19):**
- Look for **broken strands** (meat hooks) — run a cloth along the wire, it will snag on broken wires
- Check for **rust staining** — indicates internal corrosion
- Any broken strand = **replace the entire wire**. Don't wait.

**Swage fittings (pressed/rolled):**
- Look for **cracks** at the barrel, especially near the wire entry
- Check for **distortion** — the fitting should be straight and symmetrical
- Look for **rust staining** at the wire-to-fitting junction
- **Swage fittings have a finite life** — many riggers recommend replacement every 10-15 years regardless of appearance

**Sta-Lok / Norseman fittings (mechanical):**
- Can be disassembled for inspection — do this every 5 years
- Check the cone for cracks and the wire for corrosion inside the fitting
- Re-assemble with new sealant (Loctite or Duralac)
- Advantage: can be field-replaced without special tools

**Turnbuckles:**
- Check for bent bodies or studs
- Ensure cotter pins/split rings are in place (prevents them from unscrewing)
- Lubricate threads with Tefgel or lanolin annually
- Toggle must move freely — frozen toggles transmit bending loads to the wire

**Toggles:**
- Must be present at both chainplate and turnbuckle connections
- Must pivot freely on their pins
- Check pins for wear (elongated holes = replace)

**Spreaders:**
- Check tip fittings — wire should be positively retained (seized, bolted, or pop-riveted)
- Inspect for cracks at the mast attachment
- Should angle slightly upward (bisect the angle of the shroud)
- Tape or boot the tips to prevent sail chafe

### Chainplate Inspection
Chainplates anchor the rigging to the hull. They pass through the deck and bolt to internal structure.

1. Check for **water staining** at the deck penetration (indicates a leak — water will rot the core or weaken the hull)
2. Look for **cracks or distortion** in the chainplate
3. Check **bolts** — must be tight and not corroded
4. If the chainplate goes below deck: check for cracks from inside the boat
5. Rebedding: Remove, clean, re-seal with butyl tape or polyurethane sealant

## Running Rigging

Running rigging moves during sailing: halyards, sheets, control lines, reefing lines. It wears out and must be replaced regularly.

### Inspection Points
- **Chafe:** Look where lines pass through clutches, blocks, fairleads, and over spreader tips
- **UV degradation:** Lines left exposed on deck fade and lose strength
- **Core pull-through:** In double-braid lines, if the cover slides freely over the core, the line is compromised
- **Stiffness:** Old polyester line becomes stiff and hard to handle
- **Fuzz:** Surface fuzzing is normal; deep fuzzing or exposed core strands means replace

### Line Materials
| Material | Use | Lifespan | Notes |
|----------|-----|----------|-------|
| Polyester (Dacron) | Sheets, halyards, general | 5-8 years | Best value, some stretch |
| Dyneema/Spectra | Halyards, high-load | 5-10 years | Near-zero stretch, very strong, UV sensitive |
| Nylon | Anchor rode, dock lines | 5-8 years | High stretch (shock absorber), weakens when wet |
| Polypropylene | Dinghy painters, MOB line | 3-5 years | Floats, cheapest, least durable |

### Halyard Replacement
1. Tie the new halyard to the end of the old one (use a long sheet bend or tape together)
2. Pull the old halyard out from below, feeding the new one up through the mast
3. If there's no old halyard: use a messenger line — tie a small weight to fishing line, feed through the mast sheave from the top, then pull the halyard through
4. A "mouse line" left permanently in the mast makes future replacement easier

## Sail Inspection and Repair

### Pre-Season Inspection
Spread the sail on a clean, dry surface. Check:
1. **Stitching:** Look along all seams and panels for broken or chafed thread. Hold the sail up to light — broken stitches show as gaps.
2. **Fabric:** Check for thin spots (hold up to light), UV damage (chalky/brittle), or tears
3. **Leech line and foot cord:** Pull and release — should run freely
4. **Battens:** Slide them in and out, check for broken/cracked battens
5. **Hardware:** Check headboard, tack ring, clew ring, slides/slugs for wear and corrosion
6. **UV cover** (on roller furling sails): Check for stitching failure and UV degradation of the cover itself

### Emergency Sail Repair at Sea

**Sail tape (sticky-back Dacron):** The quickest repair. Apply to both sides of a tear. Works well for tears up to 12 inches if the edges can be aligned. Carry a roll of sail repair tape on every passage.

**Hand sewing — palm and needle:**
For more durable repairs. Every offshore sailor should carry a sailmaker's palm, needles, and waxed twine.

1. **Thread the needle** with waxed polyester twine (about 3 feet)
2. **Align the torn edges** — overlap slightly if fabric is missing
3. **Start 1 inch before the tear** in solid fabric
4. **Use a herringbone stitch** for clean tears:
   - Push needle through one side, 1/4" from the edge
   - Cross to the other side, push through 1/4" from the edge
   - Continue, alternating sides, making an X pattern
   - Stitches should be 1/4" to 3/8" apart
5. **Finish 1 inch past the tear** in solid fabric
6. **Tie off** with several half hitches
7. **Apply sail tape over the stitching** for reinforcement and chafe protection

**Batten pocket repair:**
If a batten pocket tears, the batten can fall out and damage the sail. Tape the torn area immediately. Sew when conditions allow.

## Winch Maintenance

Winches should be serviced at least once per season. Salt, dirt, and old grease cause them to become stiff or fail.

### Disassembly and Service
1. **Remove the drum:** Most self-tailing winches have a circlip or retaining ring on top. Remove it, then lift off the drum.
2. **Remove the pawls and springs:** Note their orientation. Take photos before disassembly!
3. **Remove all parts** — lay them out in order on a clean cloth
4. **Clean everything** with mineral spirits or winch cleaner. Use a brush to remove old grease.
5. **Inspect pawls** for wear (rounded edges = replace). Check springs for breakage.
6. **Inspect bearings** for rough spots or corrosion
7. **Lubricate:** Apply winch grease (NOT general-purpose grease) to all bearings and moving surfaces. Apply light oil to pawl springs and pivot points.
8. **Reassemble** in reverse order. Pawls must face the correct direction (they should allow the drum to turn one way and lock in the other).
9. **Test:** Spin the drum — should turn freely one way and lock the other. Test under load.

**DO NOT use WD-40 on winches.** It washes away the grease. Use only marine winch grease.

### Winch Troubleshooting
- **Stiff or hard to turn:** Old grease, corrosion, or debris. Service the winch.
- **Won't hold (slips):** Worn pawls or broken pawl springs. Replace.
- **Self-tailer not gripping:** Worn rubber stripper ring. Replace (often a standard part from the manufacturer).
- **Grinding noise:** Worn bearings or contaminated grease. Service.

## Roller Furling Maintenance

### UV Strip Care
The UV cover on the leech (and sometimes foot) of a roller furling sail takes constant sun exposure. It degrades faster than the sail itself.

- Inspect annually — crumbling or powdery material needs replacement
- UV cover replacement is cheaper than a new sail
- Sew a new UV strip before the old one fails completely

### Furling Drum Service
1. Unwrap the furling line completely
2. Check the line for chafe (it runs under load — chafed lines can break)
3. Lubricate the furling drum bearings (manufacturer recommendations vary — some use dry lubricant, some light oil)
4. Check the drum for salt crystal buildup — flush with fresh water
5. Inspect the foil sections for damage and alignment

### Furling Line Replacement
1. Tie the new line to the end of the old one
2. Unfurl the sail completely
3. Attach new line at the drum
4. Furl the sail, winding the new line onto the drum
5. Check that it fairs lead smoothly without chafe

### Common Furling Problems
- **Won't furl:** Check for a jam at the drum, halyard tension too low, or a line override on the drum
- **Partial furl / sags:** Halyard not tight enough, or forestay sag
- **UV strip on wrong side:** The UV strip must face outward when furled — check which way your sail furls

## Block and Sheave Inspection

1. Spin each sheave — should rotate freely without grinding
2. Check for cracked or deformed cheeks (side plates)
3. Inspect the shackle or attachment point for wear
4. Look for score marks on the sheave from worn lines
5. Lubricate with dry Teflon spray or light oil (not WD-40)
6. Check becket pins and clevis pins — must have retaining rings or cotter pins

## Mast and Boom Fittings

### Gooseneck
Connects the boom to the mast. Must pivot freely.
- Check for play — excessive movement indicates worn bushings
- Lubricate the pivot pin
- Check bolts that secure it to the mast

### Boom Vang / Kicker
- Check all attachment points for cracks
- Lubricate tackle or hydraulic cylinder
- Inspect blocks and pins for wear
- The vang fitting on the mast takes heavy loads — check for fatigue cracks

### Masthead
If accessible (while unstepped or by going up the mast):
- Check sheaves for wear and free rotation
- Inspect halyard exits for chafe
- Check the wind instrument/antenna mounting
- Inspect the anchor light wiring
- Check all pins and retaining clips

## Rig Tuning Basics

Proper rig tuning is essential for performance and safety. The goal is to keep the mast straight and centered under load.

### Static Tuning (At Dock)
1. **Center the mast:** Sight up the mast track — it should be vertical. Measure equal halyard lengths to each rail. Adjust cap shrouds (upper shrouds) to center.
2. **Set forestay tension:** The forestay should be taut. Tighten the backstay or babystay to remove sag.
3. **Equalize shrouds:** Both port and starboard shrouds should have equal tension at each level (uppers, lowers, intermediates).
4. **Pre-bend (optional):** Some rigs benefit from a slight aft bend for mainsail shape. Achieved by tightening the backstay and loosening the lowers slightly.

### Dynamic Tuning (Under Sail)
1. **Sail on each tack** in moderate wind
2. **Check the leeward shrouds:** They should still have some tension (not flapping loose). If loose, the rig is under-tensioned.
3. **Watch the mast sideways:** It should not bow to leeward. If it does, increase upper shroud tension.
4. **Check the forestay:** Excessive sag reduces pointing ability. Increase backstay or rig tension.

**IMPORTANT:** Never over-tension the rig. This puts excessive compression load on the mast and can cause failure. Tighten incrementally, sail-test, repeat.
