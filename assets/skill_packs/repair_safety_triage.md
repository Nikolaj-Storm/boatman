# Repair Safety Triage

## Purpose — The Safety Gate

This skill is a safety-first decision framework. Before attempting ANY repair on a boat, run through this triage. It determines whether a repair is safe to attempt, what precautions are required, and when to stop and call for professional help.

**RULE: Every repair session starts here. No exceptions.**

## Step 1 — Assess the Situation

Before touching anything, answer these questions:

### Is Anyone in Immediate Danger?
- **YES** → Address the danger first (fire, flooding, man overboard, injury)
- Refer to the **Seamanship & Emergency** skill pack
- Do NOT start repairs while an emergency is active

### Is the Boat in Immediate Danger?
- **Taking on water** → Activate bilge pumps, find and stop the leak, then assess
- **Fire** → Shut down fuel, close vents, fight the fire
- **Dragging anchor toward hazard** → Re-anchor or motor clear first
- **In a shipping channel** → Move to safety before any repair

### Can This Wait?
- If you're in a safe anchorage with no urgency, take time to plan properly
- Rushed repairs cause more damage and create new hazards
- Nighttime repairs at sea should only address immediate safety concerns

## Step 2 — Isolation and Lockout

Before working on any system, isolate it from its energy source.

### Electrical Isolation
- **Turn off the relevant breaker** at the electrical panel
- For major work: **disconnect the battery bank** (negative terminal first)
- **Verify zero voltage** with a multimeter before touching wires
- Post a note at the panel: "DO NOT ENERGIZE — WORK IN PROGRESS"
- **Shore power:** Disconnect the shore power cord at the dock end first

### Fuel Isolation
- **Close the fuel shutoff valve** at the tank before any fuel system work
- Wipe up any spilled fuel immediately — diesel has a low flash point
- **No smoking, no sparks** within 10 feet of fuel work
- Have a fire extinguisher within arm's reach
- **Ventilate the engine compartment** for 10 minutes before working

### Mechanical Isolation
- **Engine must be OFF** and ignition key removed during any engine work
- **Disable the starter** — disconnect the battery or pull the starter fuse
- Never work on a running engine unless absolutely required (e.g., checking alternator output)
- **Propeller:** Engine off, transmission in gear (prevents shaft from turning), and lock if possible

### Seawater Isolation
- **Close the relevant seacock** before disconnecting any hose below the waterline
- Verify the seacock is closed (handle perpendicular to the hose = closed)
- Have wooden plugs ready at every through-hull in case of fitting failure

### Hydraulic Isolation
- **Relieve pressure** before disconnecting any hydraulic line
- Use rags to catch residual fluid — hydraulic fluid under pressure can inject through skin
- Mark disconnected lines for correct reassembly

## Step 3 — Risk Assessment

### Severity Classification

**CRITICAL — Do Not Attempt Without Professional Help:**
- Standing rigging failure while sailing (get to port under reduced sail)
- Keel bolt concerns (structural — haul out for inspection)
- Hull breach below waterline (temporary patch only, then get to a yard)
- Fuel injection system (high-pressure — injection injury risk)
- LPG/CNG gas system leaks (explosion risk)
- Mast climbing without proper gear and a belayer
- Steering system failure at sea (rig emergency tiller, get to port)

**HIGH — Proceed with Extreme Caution:**
- Electrical work on the main battery bank (short circuit = fire/explosion)
- Engine cooling system while hot (scalding coolant)
- Through-hull replacement (must be hauled or carefully coordinated)
- Stuffing box adjustment (water ingress risk if done wrong)
- Hydraulic steering work (loss of steering capability)
- Any repair while underway in rough conditions

**MODERATE — Standard Precautions:**
- Engine oil/filter change
- Impeller replacement
- Fuel filter change (with proper bleeding)
- Electrical connections and wiring (with breaker off)
- Winch service
- Sail repairs
- Deck hardware rebedding

**LOW — Minimal Risk:**
- Cleaning and inspection
- Gelcoat touch-up
- Hose clamp tightening (above waterline)
- Line replacement
- Lubrication
- Zinc anode replacement (while hauled)

## Step 4 — Pre-Repair Checklist

Before starting any moderate or higher repair:

- [ ] Identified and isolated the energy source
- [ ] Verified isolation (tested with multimeter, checked seacock, etc.)
- [ ] Informed crew what you're doing and where
- [ ] Gathered all required tools and parts BEFORE starting
- [ ] Have backup plan if the repair fails
- [ ] Fire extinguisher accessible
- [ ] First aid kit accessible
- [ ] Bilge pump ready (for any below-waterline work)
- [ ] Adequate light for the work area
- [ ] Escape route clear (don't block your way out of the engine compartment)

## Step 5 — When to Stop

### Stop Immediately If:
- You smell fuel or propane in an enclosed space
- You see sparks near fuel or batteries
- Water ingress rate increases during a repair
- You feel unsure about the next step on a critical system
- The repair is getting worse, not better
- You're fatigued, seasick, or your hands are shaking
- Weather is deteriorating and you need to be on deck

### Get Professional Help When:
- The repair involves high-pressure fuel injection systems
- You suspect structural damage (keel, hull integrity, rudder stock)
- Electrical problems you can't trace with a multimeter
- The engine makes new/different noises after your repair
- You've attempted a fix twice and it hasn't worked
- The system is one you've never worked on before AND it's safety-critical
- Refrigerant systems (requires certification and special tools)

### Temporary vs. Permanent Repairs
Not every repair at sea needs to be permanent. It's often better to make a safe temporary fix and do the proper repair at a boatyard.

**Good temporary fixes:**
- Hose clamp on a leaking hose (get to port for proper hose replacement)
- Sail tape on a torn sail (sew properly when conditions allow)
- Wire nut + tape on a broken wire (proper crimp at the next stop)
- Wooden plug in a failed through-hull (replace the fitting when hauled)

**Bad temporary fixes:**
- Bigger fuse to stop fuse blowing (FIRE HAZARD)
- Bypassing a safety switch
- Over-tightening a stuffing box to stop a leak (shaft damage)
- Ignoring an oil leak ("it's not that bad")

## Step 6 — Post-Repair Verification

After completing any repair:

1. **Re-check all connections** — tightened, clamped, secured?
2. **Remove all tools** from the engine compartment and bilge
3. **Restore isolation** — turn breakers back on, open seacocks, reconnect batteries
4. **Test the repair** under controlled conditions before relying on it
5. **Monitor** for the first hour — check for leaks, unusual sounds, temperature
6. **Log the repair** — date, what was done, parts used, notes for next time
7. **Brief the crew** — what was fixed, what to watch for, any limitations

## Emergency Tool and Parts Kit

**Every boat should carry:**

### Tools
- Multimeter (most important diagnostic tool)
- Adjustable wrenches (2 sizes)
- Screwdriver set (Phillips and flat)
- Pliers (regular + needle-nose + wire cutters)
- Socket set (metric and/or imperial to match your engine)
- Allen key set
- Ratcheting crimp tool + heat-shrink connectors
- Hacksaw with spare blades
- Wire brush
- Duct tape + electrical tape + rigging tape
- Cable ties (assorted sizes)
- Flashlight/headlamp with spare batteries

### Spare Parts
- Engine impeller + gasket
- Fuel filters (primary + secondary)
- Engine oil + filter
- Alternator belt
- Fuses (assorted, matching your panel)
- Hose clamps (assorted sizes)
- Wooden through-hull plugs (every size on your boat)
- Zinc anodes
- Electrical wire (10, 14, 18 AWG tinned)
- Heat-shrink butt connectors
- Waterproof grease
- Penetrating oil (PB Blaster or equivalent)
- Sealant (tube of 4200 or Sikaflex)
- Sail repair tape
- Epoxy (two-part, waterproof)
