# Seamanship & Emergency Procedures

## Essential Knots

### Bowline — The King of Knots
Creates a fixed loop that won't slip or jam. Use for: attaching sheets to sails, rescue loops, securing to a post.

**How to tie:**
1. Make a small loop in the standing part (the "rabbit hole")
2. Pass the free end up through the loop (rabbit comes out of the hole)
3. Go around behind the standing part (rabbit goes around the tree)
4. Pass back down through the small loop (rabbit goes back in the hole)
5. Pull tight — the loop should be fixed and the knot secure

**Memory aid:** "The rabbit comes out of the hole, goes around the tree, and back down the hole."

### Cleat Hitch
Secures a line to a horn cleat. Used for dock lines, halyards, sheets.

**How to tie:**
1. Take one full turn around the base of the cleat (far horn first)
2. Make a figure-eight over the horns: over one horn, under the other, over again
3. Finish with a locking hitch: twist the line into a loop, slip it over the horn
4. The locking hitch should be opposite to the last figure-eight pass

**Common mistake:** Don't pile up turns — one full turn, two figure-eights, one locking hitch is sufficient.

### Clove Hitch
Quick temporary fastening to a post, rail, or ring. Not secure for heavy or variable loads — always back up with a half hitch.

**How to tie:**
1. Wrap the line around the post
2. Cross over the standing part
3. Wrap around the post again
4. Tuck the free end under the second wrap
5. Add a half hitch for security

### Sheet Bend
Joins two lines of different diameters. Essential for extending lines.

**How to tie:**
1. Make a bight (U-shape) in the thicker line
2. Pass the thinner line up through the bight from underneath
3. Wrap around both parts of the bight
4. Tuck the thin line under itself (but over the bight)
5. Pull tight — both free ends should be on the same side

**Double sheet bend:** For very different diameters, wrap around twice before tucking. Much more secure.

### Rolling Hitch
Attaches a line to a loaded line or pole with grip. Essential for taking the load off a jammed winch or transferring loads.

**How to tie:**
1. Wrap the line around the pole/loaded line twice, going in the direction of the pull
2. Cross over both wraps
3. Make one more wrap on the other side of the cross
4. Tuck under the crossing turn
5. Pull on the standing part — the hitch should grip firmly

### Trucker's Hitch
Creates a mechanical advantage for tensioning lines. Great for lashing, securing loads, and tightening tarps.

**How to tie:**
1. Tie a fixed loop in the standing part (use a slip knot or alpine butterfly)
2. Pass the free end around the attachment point
3. Thread the free end back through the loop
4. Pull — you now have a 2:1 or 3:1 mechanical advantage
5. Secure with two half hitches

## Line Handling and Splicing

### Eye Splice (Three-Strand Rope)
Creates a permanent loop in the end of a line. Stronger than any knot.

1. Unlay 6-8 inches of the three strands
2. Form the eye to the desired size
3. Tuck the middle strand under one strand of the standing part (against the lay)
4. Tuck the left strand under the next strand to the left
5. Flip the work over; tuck the remaining strand under the remaining strand
6. Continue tucking each strand over one, under one — at least 3 full tucks
7. Trim the ends and tape or whip

### Whipping a Line End
Prevents the end of a line from unraveling.

1. Lay a loop of whipping twine along the rope end
2. Wrap tightly away from the end, covering the loop
3. After 10-15 wraps, pass the working end through the loop
4. Pull the other end of the loop to draw the working end under the wraps
5. Trim both ends flush

## Anchoring

### Setting an Anchor
1. **Choose your spot:** Check the chart for depth, bottom type (sand/mud = good, rock/weed = poor)
2. **Calculate scope:** Pay out 5:1 rode-to-depth ratio minimum (7:1 for heavy weather). Include tidal height.
3. **Approach slowly** upwind or upcurrent
4. **Lower (don't throw) the anchor** when stopped
5. **Back down slowly** while paying out rode
6. **Set the anchor:** Once full scope is out, increase reverse throttle to dig in
7. **Take bearings** on two fixed objects to monitor for dragging
8. **Set an anchor alarm** if available

### Dragging Anchor
**Signs:** Bearings change, GPS position shifts, depth changes unexpectedly.

**What to do:**
1. Start the engine immediately
2. Motor forward slowly to reduce strain
3. Retrieve rode until over the anchor, try to reset
4. If it won't hold: retrieve fully, move to a new spot
5. Consider a different anchor type or adding more scope

### Fouled Anchor
If the anchor won't come up:
1. Motor directly over the anchor
2. Cleat the rode short and motor slowly in the opposite direction from the set
3. Try motoring in a circle around the anchor
4. Last resort: attach a trip line or use a chain hook to pull from the crown

## Man Overboard (MOB) Procedures

**SAFETY: Practice this procedure regularly. In a real MOB, every second counts.**

### Immediate Actions
1. **SHOUT "MAN OVERBOARD"** and point at the person continuously
2. **Throw flotation** — life ring, cushion, anything that floats
3. **Press MOB button** on GPS/chartplotter
4. **Assign a spotter** — one person points at the MOB at all times
5. **Start the engine** (if sailing)

### Recovery Maneuver (Quick-Stop)
1. Immediately tack or turn toward the MOB
2. Do NOT let the MOB out of sight
3. Approach from downwind, slowly
4. Stop the boat alongside the MOB (engine in neutral when close — propeller danger)
5. Use a swim ladder, halyard, or lifting sling to get them aboard

### Hypothermia Risk
Cold water kills. At 15°C/59°F water temperature, a person may survive 1-6 hours. At 5°C/41°F, survival time drops to 15-45 minutes.

## Emergency Signaling

### VHF Distress Call (MAYDAY)
For immediate danger to life or vessel:
1. Check radio is on Channel 16, high power
2. Press and hold transmit
3. Say: **"MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY"**
4. **"This is [vessel name] (×3)"**
5. **"MAYDAY [vessel name]"**
6. **"My position is [lat/lon or bearing and distance from landmark]"**
7. **"Nature of distress: [sinking/fire/medical emergency]"**
8. **"Number of persons on board: [number]"**
9. **"Require immediate assistance"**
10. **"Over"**

### PAN-PAN (Urgency, not immediate danger)
Same format but say "PAN-PAN" three times instead of MAYDAY.

### Visual Distress Signals
- **Red handheld flares:** Good for 1 minute, visible 3-5 miles. Light downwind, hold at arm's length, look away.
- **Red parachute flares:** Shoots to 300m, visible 25+ miles. Fire at 45° angle downwind.
- **Orange smoke signals:** Daytime use, visible 3-5 miles. Good for helicopter rescues.
- **EPIRB:** Activate for serious emergencies only — sends satellite distress signal with GPS position. Floats, operates for 48+ hours.

## Heavy Weather Procedures

### Heaving-To
The classic storm tactic for sailboats. The boat drifts slowly at 50-60° to the wind, relatively comfortable.

1. Tack without releasing the jib sheet (back the jib)
2. Lash the tiller to leeward (or turn the wheel to windward)
3. Adjust mainsail — ease or reduce as needed
4. The boat should settle into a balanced, slow drift
5. The backed jib pushes the bow off, the rudder pushes it up — they cancel out

### Sea Anchor (Bow)
A parachute-like device deployed from the bow. Keeps the bow into the waves.
- Best for: multihulls, motorboats, waiting out a storm
- Deploy from the bow with a long rode (10× boat length minimum)
- Attach a trip line for retrieval

### Drogue (Stern)
A drag device towed from the stern to slow the boat in following seas.
- Best for: monohull sailboats running before a storm
- Prevents surfing and broaching
- Jordan Series Drogue: many small cones on a long line — gold standard

## Fire Fighting on Board

### Engine Room Fire
1. **Shut down the engine** immediately
2. **Close the fuel shutoff valve** at the tank
3. **Close all engine room vents/hatches** — starve it of oxygen
4. **Discharge a CO2 or dry chemical extinguisher** through a port (don't open the engine room fully)
5. **Do NOT use water** on a fuel fire
6. If not controlled quickly: **prepare to abandon ship**

### Galley Fire
1. Turn off the stove fuel supply
2. Cover with a fire blanket or lid
3. Use a dry chemical extinguisher if needed
4. Never throw water on a grease fire

### Electrical Fire
1. Disconnect the battery bank (battery switch OFF)
2. Use a CO2 extinguisher — it won't damage electronics
3. Never use water on an electrical fire

## First Aid at Sea

### Hypothermia
**Signs:** Shivering, confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination.

1. Get the person out of the water/wind
2. Remove wet clothing, replace with dry layers
3. Wrap in blankets, sleeping bags — insulate from cold surfaces below
4. Apply heat to core (armpits, neck, groin) — warm water bottles, body heat
5. Give warm sweet drinks (NOT alcohol)
6. Do NOT rub extremities — this pushes cold blood to the core
7. **Severe hypothermia** (no shivering, unconscious): handle very gently — rough handling can cause cardiac arrest. Evacuate immediately.

### Severe Bleeding
1. Apply direct pressure with a clean cloth
2. Keep pressing — do not lift to check for 10-15 minutes
3. Elevate the wound above the heart if possible
4. If bleeding won't stop: apply a tourniquet above the wound (last resort for limbs)
5. Seek medical evacuation

### Fractures
1. Immobilize the limb in the position found
2. Splint using any rigid material (winch handle, battens, rolled magazine)
3. Pad the splint for comfort
4. Check circulation below the splint (color, warmth, pulse)
5. Give pain relief if available
6. Evacuate for confirmed fractures

### Seasickness
- Move to the cockpit (fresh air, horizon visible)
- Lie down if possible, eyes closed or fixed on the horizon
- Stay hydrated — small sips of water
- Ginger (capsules, chews, or tea) can help
- Scopolamine patches work but must be applied before departure
- Severe cases: risk dehydration — monitor fluid intake

## Navigation Without Electronics

### Compass Navigation
- Your magnetic compass is the backup when electronics fail
- Know your compass deviation (listed on the deviation card)
- Convert: True → Magnetic → Compass (add West deviation, subtract East)
- Take bearings on known landmarks to establish your position

### Dead Reckoning
When you can't see landmarks or use GPS:
1. Note your last known position
2. Record course steered (compass heading)
3. Estimate speed (knot log or experience)
4. Account for current and leeway
5. Plot your estimated position on the chart
6. Update every hour minimum

### Rule of Thumb — Distance Off
- If you can see individual bricks on a building: ~1 mile
- If you can see windows: ~2 miles
- If you can see the building but not features: ~5 miles
- Lighthouse visible on horizon: distance ≈ 1.17 × √(height in feet) nautical miles
